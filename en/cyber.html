Memo Explanations

A simplified explanation with everyday analogies for everything in the memo's cybersecurity section.

01. What Is Cybersecurity?

Cybersecurity Cybersecurity is the state of protection against criminal or unauthorized use of your electronic data. In other words: everything you do to keep strangers away from your photos, messages, bank accounts, and files.

🏠 Everyday analogy: Think of your data as your home. Cybersecurity is the locks on the doors, the window curtains, the alarm bell, and your habit of never handing the house key to a stranger who knocks. No single lock is enough — it's a set of habits and tools working together.
⚔️

What is a cyberattack? Cyberattack

An attempt by attackers to damage or destroy a network or computer system. Like a thief trying to break down your front door — but over the internet.

🔑

What is a password? Password

A secret word or phrase you use to access your accounts. It is the "key to your digital home" — which is why it must never be easy to guess or reused across sites.

🔐

What is encryption? Encryption

Converting information into unreadable code to prevent unauthorized access. Like writing a message in a secret language that only someone with the "decryption key" can understand.

💾

What is a backup? Backup

Copying your original files and storing them somewhere else (an external drive or the cloud). If your device is lost or infected by a virus, your files stay safe.

🪪

What is authentication? Authentication

Proving that you really are the account owner — and the memo's accepted example is the username Username. Like showing your ID card before entering a building.

🧑

The biggest threat to cybersecurity?

Surprise: it is not technology, but the human factor The human factor! Most breaches succeed because someone clicked a suspicious link or gave their information to someone who did not deserve trust.

💡 Bottom line: Any secure system must achieve all three together: data that is confidential so strangers cannot see it, intact so nobody tampers with it, and available to its owners when needed. If one side falls, the whole protection falls.
📌 Exam note: The official training material sometimes writes the model's name in the order CAI Triad Model instead of CIA — don't be confused if you see either order in the exam; they mean the same thing: confidentiality, integrity, and availability.

How do you protect yourself? Regular backups are your strongest weapon: if your files are saved elsewhere, the blackmail loses its value.

04. Who Are Hackers? And Are They All Villains?

A hacker Hacker is a person who uses a computer to gain unauthorized access to other people's devices and steal their personal information. But not everyone with hacking skills is a criminal — they are classified by "hat color":

🤍 White Hat White Hat

The ethical hacker

Uses their skills to run "penetration tests", uncover security vulnerabilities, and report them to the organization's owner for pay — like a locksmith you hire to check your home's doors.

🖤 Black Hat Black Hat

The cybercriminal

Breaks into systems for criminal goals: data theft, blackmail, sabotage. This is the "thief" you protect yourself from, and the one meant by the memo's definition of a "computer hacker".

🩶 Grey Hat Grey Hat

Somewhere in between

Breaks into systems without permission (which is wrong) but usually doesn't aim to cause harm — and may report the vulnerability after discovering it.

🩵 Blue Hat Blue Hat

Not a hacker!

The official material classifies hackers into only three categories (black, white, and grey) and never mentions a "blue hat" — which is why the accepted answer to "Who is not a hacker?" is: the blue hat.

🎯 For the exam remember: Blue Hat → not a hacker (not mentioned in the curriculum)

07. From the Official Material — What the Memo Doesn't Cover

The memo's questions focus almost entirely on the first chapter of the official material, while the material spans 6 full chapters. These are the most important extra topics covered officially — new exam questions may come from them:

🧮

The risk equation

The official material distinguishes three concepts: a security vulnerability Vulnerability (a weakness in a device or program), and a threat Threat (whoever tries to exploit it), summed up in the equation: Threats + Vulnerability = Risks.

🏛️

The relevant Egyptian bodies

EG-CERT: the National Computer Emergency Readiness Team (established 2009 within the National Telecom Regulatory Authority) handles cyber incidents. And ESCC: the Egyptian Supreme Cybersecurity Council (2014) sets the national strategic plan.

🐴

More malware

Trojans: hide inside a seemingly useful program and don't copy themselves; instead they open a "backdoor". Spyware: collects your information and sends it to the attacker. Rootkits: hide from antivirus software and allow remote control of your device.

💥

More attacks

Zero-day: exploiting a new vulnerability for which no patch has been released yet. Evil Twin: a fake Wi-Fi network carrying the original network's name. Plus email address spoofing, caller ID spoofing, and exploit kit injection.

🚦

Traffic Light Protocol (TLP)

A color system for sharing sensitive information: red (recipient only), amber (limited sharing within the organization), green (within the same community, no public release), white (unlimited sharing).

⚖️

Egyptian legislation

The Anti-Cyber and Information Technology Crimes Law 175 of 2018, the Personal Data Protection Law 151 of 2020, the Child Law (12/1996 amended by 126/2008), and Article 57 of the Constitution (inviolability of private life).

📵

Cyberbullying and child protection

A full chapter on bullying types (exclusion, catfishing, cyberstalking, doxing, impersonation...) and the signs a child is being targeted, with the 16000 hotline for reporting online risks to children.

🛍️

Scam websites and their signs

Types: deceptive websites, fraudulent shopping with a fake link Spoofed URL, lottery contests, and scare sites Scareware. Their signs: emotionally charged wording, poor design, and spelling and grammar mistakes.
